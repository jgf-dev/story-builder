**Step 1: Analyze Components and Characters**
Carefully identify and define all components and characters mentioned in the specifications, use the findings of your search to strengthen them, fill in gaps, or add touches of realism.
For each character, build a detailed profile structured as a **Character Brief** covering five interlocking dimensions:

### 1. Core Identity (Who they are)

- **Background** — formative experiences, family/community, socioeconomic context, education
- **Personality matrix** — 3-5 defining traits, including at least one virtue and one flaw that are **two sides of the same coin** (e.g. loyalty → stubbornness, idealism → naivety)
- **Voice & register** — how they speak (vocabulary level, cadence, idiolect, favorite filler words, silence patterns)
- **Physical presence** — not just appearance, but how they inhabit space (posture, gesture patterns, energy level)

### 2. Agency & Trajectory (What drives them)

- **Surface goal** — what they *think* they want (achievable, tangible)
- **Deep need** — what they *actually* need (often unknown to them, linked to their flaw)
- **The Lie They Believe** — a core misconception about themselves or the world that the story will challenge
- **Arc direction** — how this character will change (or stubbornly resist change) across the narrative

### 3. Interior Landscape (What they long for)

- **Desires** — conscious wants (romantic, social, material, experiential)
- **Fears** — what they're running from or avoiding (should connect to the Lie)
- **Core wound** — the originating event or condition that shaped their flaw (may be subtle, not traumatic)
- **Secret** — something they'd never admit aloud (could be trivial or profound)

### 4. Relational Dynamics (How they move through the world)

- **Interaction style** — dominant/submissive/avoidant/assertive; how they enter a room, handle conflict, show affection
- **How others see them** — external perception vs. internal reality (the gap is where tension lives)
- **Whom they orbit** — specific patterns in relationships (do they chase? withdraw? fix? perform?)
- **Social status & navigation** — where they sit in the story's power/status hierarchy

### 5. The Unexpected (Why this character hasn't been done before)

- **Trope audit** — identify which YA trope this character *could* be, then name the specific subversion or twist
- **Contradiction engine** — at least one internal contradiction that generates conflict naturally (e.g. a boy who craves intimacy but can't tolerate vulnerability; a jock whose deepest skill is lace-making)
- **Tell-tale detail** — one concrete, sensory-specific habit, object, or memory that makes them feel like a real person (not a construct)
- **Threshold moment** — a single past event (shown or implied) that sets their story in motion

**Step 2: Create Interaction Graph**

**Step 3: Define the Plot**
Think outside the box to ensure a captivating result. Write out explicitly:

- The beginning state

- The sequence of events (plot beats) in order

**Step 4: Outline Chapters**
Based on your plot, define the chapters. For each chapter:

- Give it a title
- Create a bullet-point list of necessary components to include
- Create an interaction sub-graph based on the first one, but with only the events that occur in the chapter.
